Inside Truco Eval

Truco Paulista is a Brazilian card game of hidden hands, silent partners and loud bets, where a single hand can go from 1 point to 12. Truco Eval sits two LLMs at the table as rival teams and logs every decision down to the token. This page walks through how it works, one diagram at a time, then shows what 200 matches between DeepSeek-v4.1-flash and GLM-5.3-flash turned up. Section 5 takes the same harness to Kaggle's free compute for a round robin between five low-cost models.

1

How Truco is played

Four players in two teams, three cards each. A hand is best of three tricks, and on any turn a team can raise what the hand is worth.

1.1The table

Partners sit opposite each other. Team A holds seats 0 and 2 and Team B holds seats 1 and 3, so turns always alternate between the teams. The dealer moves one seat after every hand, and the player after the dealer, the mão, leads the first trick. In the benchmark each team is one model: the same LLM plays both of its seats, with a separate call for every turn.

1.2The cards

The deck has 40 cards: 4, 5, 6, 7, Q, J, K, A, 2 and 3 in each suit. After the deal one card is turned face up, the vira. The next rank after it in the cycle becomes the manilhas, the four strongest cards of the hand, ranked by suit. Every other card ranks by rank alone, so two cards of the same rank tie. Pick a vira and watch the order change.

1.3Tricks and hands

In each trick every player plays one card, and the team with the strongest card wins the trick. That player leads the next one. Win two tricks and you win the hand. When the top card is tied between the two teams the trick is a parda, and the rules below decide the hand. There is no need to follow suit: any card can be played at any time.

1.4Betting: Truco, Seis, Nove, Doze

Every hand starts at 1 point. On their own turn, before playing a card, a player can raise: Truco to 3, then Seis to 6, Nove to 9 and Doze to 12. Play stops and the other team must answer: accept, decline and give up the stake as it stood before the call, or raise back to the next rung. After an accept, only the team that accepted may raise next. Play both teams below.

1.5Special hands and table talk

A team on 11 points plays a mão de onze: the partners see each other's cards, then choose to play for a fixed 3 points or forfeit 1. At 11–11 the hand is a mão de ferro, played blind with the match at stake. On any turn a player may also say up to 200 characters to the table. Everyone hears it, and nobody checks it.

3

How the harness runs a match

A deterministic engine, a thin model adapter, and a runner that prompts one seat at a time. Every decision is a fresh, stateless LLM call that is parsed strictly, checked for leaks and logged.

3.1Three layers

The engine knows nothing about LLMs and the model adapter knows nothing about Truco. The runner joins them. The model interface, sampler and parser base classes come from Google DeepMind's Game Arena harness, used as a pinned dependency rather than a fork. Click a box to see what it holds.

3.2The match loop

The runner asks the engine whose turn it is, what that seat may see and which moves are legal. Then it asks that seat's model to choose. The engine applies the move and emits events, and the loop repeats until a team reaches 12. Press play to follow one real decision: seat 2 calling Truco in seed 0.

3.3One decision up close

Each call is a single turn with no chat history. The system message is the full rules text, resent on every call. The user message is the seat's view of the game plus the exact legal moves, and the history in it replaces memory. Hover the labels to see each part of a real prompt: seat 0's first move in seed 0.

3.4When a reply is illegal

The parser reads only the last Final Answer: line, and it must map to exactly one legal move. It forgives spelling (ASCII suits, Portuguese words, a bare rank) but never ambiguity. If it fails, the model is asked once more with its reply quoted back. A second failure plays a uniformly random legal move, and any talk in the failed reply is dropped.

3.5Information isolation

A seat's prompt is rendered only from what the rules let it see: its own cards, the vira, cards already played, the public history and the table talk. As a tripwire, the runner also scans every prompt for any card that seat must not see before the move is applied, and aborts the match if it finds one. Re-checked for this page against a fresh engine: all 16,323 prompts in the run are clean.

3.6Duplicate matches

Luck of the deal is the biggest source of noise in a card game. So each seed is played twice with identical deals, once with each model on the even seats. The deal for hand k depends only on the seed and k, so the cards stay the same however differently the two matches are played. Random fallback moves come from their own seeded stream, so they never shift the deals. Toggle the seating.

3.7What gets logged

Every match writes four files, and the whole run rolls up into one report. The engine log is deterministic: replaying the logged moves from the seed rebuilds it byte for byte. Re-checked for this page: all 200 matches replay exactly. Click a file to see a real excerpt.

4

What 200 matches showed

DeepSeek-v4.1-flash and GLM-5.3-flash finished level, got there in opposite ways, and both lost hundreds of moves to a token cap.

DeepSeek-v4.1-flash (Novita)GLM-5.3-flash (SiliconFlow)

4.1A statistical tie

DeepSeek won 103 matches to 97. If both models were equally strong, a split at least this lopsided would happen 72% of the time. The 95% interval on DeepSeek's win rate runs from 44.6% to 58.3%. Drag the slider to see how many wins it would take to call a winner.

4.2The deal decides most seeds

Each seed is one deal sequence played from both sides. On 65 of 100 seeds the result flipped with the seating: whichever model held the better cards won. Only 35 seeds went to the same model both times, 19 to 16. That is the skill signal, and it is small. The even seats won 109 of 200 matches (54.5%); seat 0 leads the first trick of every match.

4.3Most hands never reach a showdown

Of 1,863 hands, 54% ended when a team declined a raise, and only 24% were played to the last card. When a hand was played out, DeepSeek won 341 of 454.

4.4Two styles, same score

Both models raise about as often (18.9% and 17.5% of the turns where a raise was legal). DeepSeek raises on cards: 58% of its raises hold a manilha, and it declines 60% of the raises it faces. GLM raises on a weak hand more often (34% of its raises against 20%), waits for trick 2, and talks on almost every turn.

The two styles pay off in different places. GLM's weak raises mostly win because DeepSeek gives up. When DeepSeek stays in, GLM loses 32 of 39 showdowns. Declines cost DeepSeek 381 points net, and it more than wins them back in the other hands that finish at a stake of 3 or more (+480).

4.5The hidden variable: a token cap

Each call could use at most 16,384 output tokens, reasoning included. DeepSeek hit that cap on 17.8% of its calls and GLM on 10.5%. A capped call comes back with no answer, so it counts as illegal: 96% of DeepSeek's 1,512 illegal replies and 93% of GLM's 857 were empty answers after using the full budget.

Replies that fail twice become random moves, and those leak into the behavior metrics. 39% of GLM's folds (32 of 82) were random picks, not decisions.

4.6Same result, different bill

DeepSeek spent 2.9× as much as GLM for a result inside the noise, so GLM won 2.8× as many matches per dollar. GLM's calls took longer on average, but that reflects its provider's speed as much as the model, which is why the benchmark logs the provider that served every response.

4.7Caveats

Five cheap models on Kaggle

Kaggle Benchmarks gives every account $10 of free model inference a day. Since 29 September the same harness has played a round robin there between five low-cost models, one batch per day of quota: 280 matches so far.

5.1Running on Kaggle's quota

Kaggle runs a benchmark task as a notebook on its own servers, and the models answer through Kaggle's Model Proxy instead of OpenRouter. A small adapter gives the proxy the same model interface the runner already uses, so the engine, prompts, parser and logs are unchanged. The quota is $10 a day and $100 a month, and the daily allowance refills 24 hours after its first spend. A batch starts a match only if the money spent so far, plus the expected cost of every match still in play, stays under $9.60, so a batch never runs out of quota mid-match. Since 1 October a scheduled job launches the next batch as soon as the quota refills.

5.2The standings

The two Gemini flash-lite models won about two thirds of their matches, 74 and 73 of 112, and gpt-oss-20b won under a third. Those three results are clear of a coin flip. qwen3-next-80b and gpt-5.4-nano sit in between, and their intervals overlap almost completely, so their order is not settled yet. As in the 200-match run, the even seats had an edge: they won 160 of 280 matches (57%).

5.3Head to head

The pairings do not form a clean ladder. gemini-3.1-flash-lite beat gemini-3.5-flash-lite 18–10, yet 3.5 finishes ahead overall because it beat gpt-5.4-nano 24–4. gpt-5.4-nano in turn beat qwen3-next-80b 18–10 and still ranks below it on total wins.

5.4Most hands are played out

These five models give up far less often than DeepSeek and GLM did. Of 2,466 hands, 65% were played to the last card, against 24% in the 200-match run, and only 23% ended on a declined raise, against 54%. Fewer raises are refused, so more hands reach a showdown at a stake of 3 or more.

5.5Risk profiles

Two models play a loud game. qwen3-next-80b raises on 44% of the turns where it may and gpt-oss-20b on 36%, about twice DeepSeek's and GLM's rate, and close to half of their raises come on a weak hand. The Gemini models raise rarely, 7% and 4% of the time, and mostly on strong cards: 63% of gemini-3.5-flash-lite's raises hold a manilha. gpt-5.4-nano almost never raises, 1% of the time, and accepts 76% of the raises it faces.

Facing a raise, qwen3-next-80b raises back on 22% of them, and the Geminis and gpt-5.4-nano almost never do. gpt-oss-20b folds on 5.4% of the turns where folding is legal, more than any other model here; the Geminis fold on 0.1%. qwen3-next-80b talks on almost every turn and says "bluff" 55 times in 112 matches; GLM said it 51 times in 200.

5.6Do weak raises pay?

Against these opponents a weak-hand raise works less often than it did for GLM. DeepSeek gave up on 228 of GLM's 288 weak raises (79%). Opponents gave up on 116 of qwen3-next-80b's 290 (40%) and on 75 of gpt-oss-20b's 245 (31%). When the hand went to a showdown, both lost more than they won: qwen 48 won and 55 lost, gpt-oss 37 won and 70 lost.

5.7Where the points come from

Each model's points split by how the hand ended. gpt-oss-20b's biggest leak is folding, which gives away 3.9 points a match. qwen3-next-80b collects from declined raises (+1.0) and from hands played out at 3 (+1.0), then gives back 2.2 a match by folding. gpt-5.4-nano accepts most raises and then loses those hands: −2.9 points a match in hands played out at a stake of 3. The Geminis gain most from their opponents' folds.

5.8Almost every reply was legal

The parser problems of the 200-match run barely appear here. gpt-oss-20b answered legally on the first try 97.4% of the time and the other four on 99.5% or more, against 83.5% for DeepSeek and 89.9% for GLM. Two decisions in 280 matches fell back to a random move, both gpt-oss-20b's, so the behavior figures above are the models' own choices.

5.9What a win costs

The proxy reports a cost for every call. gpt-5.4-nano is the cheapest by far at 2.4 cents a match, which makes it the best value on the table: 17.8 wins per dollar, against 9 to 11 for the other four. qwen3-next-80b scores the most points per hand, 1.43, and still wins fewer than half its matches.

5.10The token cap again

gpt-oss-20b is the one model here that reasons at length: about 4,150 output tokens per call, against 150 to 190 for the Gemini and GPT nano models. It hit the 16,384-token cap 58 times. As with DeepSeek and GLM in section 4.5, a capped call comes back with no answer, and those empty replies are 56 of its 87 illegal replies. The other four models produced 18 illegal replies between them in more than 19,000 calls.
